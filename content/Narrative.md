One person can say something like opiate [[Addiction]] or cancer is "the best thing that ever happened to me" while another person might say that it was the worst thing that ever happened to them.

This is the importance of narrative.

The problem with [[Trauma]] is that it doesn't feel like something in the past. Technically, it isn't It lives with you in some way. In the most drastic case, in something like [[Post Traumatic Stress Disorder|PTSD]], we get flashbacks. You literally get transported into the past. Your brain receives sensory input from memories. This isn't just the case with flashbacks. A lot of people feel frozen in time after their trauma. They still feel controlled by something that could have happened years ago. It defines their present and determines their future destiny.

**Neuroanatomy**
The [[Hippocampus]] is where [[Memory|Memories]] live. Damage results in amnesia. People can't form new memories, or they lose track of old memories.

The [[Posterior Cingulate Cortex]] tells us where we are, integrates information, and adds meaning, planning, and action.

In people who can't move past their [[Trauma]], the most activity we see is not in the [[Hippocampus]], it's in the [[Posterior Cingulate Cortex|PCC]]. These people are literally managing their trauma in the present moment even though something might have happened years ago.
When the [[Posterior Cingulate Cortex|PCC]] gets damaged, you go into a coma. The features of a coma: you aren't here anymore. You can't act or pay attention to stimuli.

People who are actively in the throes of [[Trauma]] (*saying things like "opiate addiction is ruining my life*), they have activity in the [[Posterior Cingulate Cortex|PCC]]. The people who move past their trauma (*saying things like, "opiate addiction is the best thing that ever happened to me*), their brain activity moves from the [[Posterior Cingulate Cortex|PCC]] to the [[Hippocampus]]. So, in a literal neuroscientific sense, they are putting the [[Trauma]] from the present back into their [[Memory|Memories]].

As people complete treatment, activity shifts from the [[Posterior Cingulate Cortex|PCC]] to the [[Hippocampus]].

If we're "stuck" in time with our [[Trauma]], how do we shift it to the past? Narrative. Integrating [[Trauma]] into a broader narrative of your life allows you to move past it and heal.

Example: negative emotional energy (intense sense of hopelessness and powerlessness) gets injected into your creative work. Without that, you wouldn't have become who you are.

How a narrative is formed:
The brain stitches together a sequence of events which are temporally linked.

Example exercise: "Tell us the story of you and your [[Addiction]]. If you had to write a story about you and -this [[Addiction]]-, what would it look like?"

When we tend to think about addictions, we think about them as negative things. However, the reason people get [[Addiction|Addicted]] is because the thing does something that is very positive.

As the person tells the story, the main thing is that their [[Mind]] is organizing these temporal events.

The goal of developing a narrative is to literally move those traumatic experiences from the [[Posterior Cingulate Cortex|PCC]] over to the [[Hippocampus]], to literally put it behind us.

We know from [[Psychotherapy]] that, as we tell these stories, there needs to be a certain level of emotional involvement as we are recalling these events. [[Sigmund Freud|Freud]] discovered that if you aren't emotionally engaged while recalling and talking about something, no healing goes on. Emotion is important for processing and putting things behind us. In [[Psychotherapy]], we'll have moments of catharsis. It could be crying, anger, shame, guilt, etc.

In treatment methodologies like [[Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing|EMDR]], we recall [[Trauma|traumatic]] memories but pay attention to something else (like, following someone's finger). This does something to the way our emotions get activated so they don't overwhelm us.

The real challenge with [[Trauma]] is that the [[Trauma]] itself affects you in a way that makes it difficult for you to form the narrative. It attacks your brain's ability to actually create narratives. Remember, the key thing about a narrative is a sequence of events with an emotional activation associated with them. But when we experience [[Trauma]], our brain adapts by numbing things. As we create [[Hemispheric Lateralization]], we start to move away from our emotions. Our emotions become things that are overwhelming and avoided at all costs.

Your brain is stopping you from moving on. You need to do is re-experience that emotion, combine it with the event, vent it, and put it past you. But since the brain is numb by our [[Trauma]], it makes it difficult to engage in this process.

Spiritual and religious traditions are a way for us to organize the narratives of our life. We can use organizing principles to explain and understand what's happening in life.
