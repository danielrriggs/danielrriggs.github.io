Alias: attachment style

Formulated by John Bowlby observing how kids were playing. Some kids were clingy with their caregiver. Others were more adventurous. He found that many of the clingier kids had some kind of separation from their caregivers (because parents traveled a lot, were sick, etc.). Very few of the healthy kids had some kind of separation.

If we are separated from our caregivers in some way we are eight times as likely to be clingy and not be able to form relationships with other kids at the time.

Mary Ainsworth tested Bowlby's theories with an experiment called the "strange situation".
- **Parent and child enter the unfamiliar room.** The child is encouraged to explore and play.
- **Stranger enters,** talks with the parent, and then gradually approaches the child.
- **Parent leaves the room,** leaving the child with the stranger.
- **Parent returns,** comforting the child if necessary, while the stranger leaves.
- **Parent leaves again,** leaving the child completely alone.
- **Stranger returns** and tries to comfort the child.
- **Parent returns** and the stranger leaves.

Kids watch the parent to see how they interact with the strangers.

The terms to classify groups of behaviors in attachment theory are fluid. There's no consensus of terms in how we classify the different ways people respond, which we call "attachment styles". Lots of research has been done, but different papers say different things.

We'll cover four basic attachment styles:

1) **Secure Attachment**
Kids are confident and exploratory. The world isn't fundamentally a dangerous place. These kids take the parents cues. If mom/dad is okay with the stranger, the kid is okay, too. When the parent leaves the room, securely attached kids get upset, because they know the parent is the source of safety. So, they start to cry. The moment the parent comes back into the room, the securely attached child calms down very quickly. They will cry for less than 5-10 seconds.

Some people can calm themselves down very quickly. Securely attached children are able to emotionally regulate themselves.

Non-securely attached kids' brains are different. In a securely attached kid, the amount of time it takes for the negative emotional circuitry to calm down is very low. For some people, once the negative emotional circuitry (things like the [[Amygdala]]) activates, it takes minutes if not hours or even days to calm down.

The ability for our negative emotions to equilibrate and return to a baseline is influenced by our attachment style.

Securely attached kids were supportively mirrored growing up. For these kids, the world is a safe place. Even if something bad happens, because mom / dad is there, it's not that big of a deal. If you were a child who learned that if you fell down, someone would pick you up, that the world is fundamentally a safe and secure place, you can be confident when you're facing uncertainty. You can afford to take risks. You can afford to form relationships with people because fundamentally, human beings are pretty decent people. You have fundamental faith in humanity.

As adults, securely attached people can form romantic relationships. If something bad happens and we break up, they can bounce back better. They can form healthy relationships because they know what they're feeling and are able to regulate their own emotions. So, they aren't relying on the other person as a crutch to regulate their emotions. They can soothe themselves.

People who are securely attached are fundamentally not very [[Anxiety|anxious]] about relationships. If they don't hear from their boyfriend / girlfriend for a day or two, they don't get too upset. They're comfortable with some amount of space because they have faith in the other person.

Securely attached people are **low avoidance**. They're comfortable with intimacy - physical or emotional. This kind of intimacy is not something to be scared of. If I have faith in people, I can afford to be vulnerable with them. But, if they are not to be trusted, then I can't be intimate with them.

2) **Anxious Attachment**
Anxiously attached kids aren't very exploratory. Need encouragement from mom  to explore. When the stranger comes in, they run back to mom. They get very dysregulated when mom leaves. Even when mom comes back, they continue to cry; they cling to her. The child may never start playing with the toys again.

In adulthood, this looks like people who grow up when there's variable responses of caregiving. The world is a fundamentally unsafe place. I can't count on the world to protect me if I fall, feel hurt, or notice me if I feel hurt.

These kids' physiology starts to wire differently. They get wired to high level of [[Anxiety]] and sensitivity to arousal. Benign things in the environment can trigger a danger response. 

They'll engage in a lot of behaviors to be noticed by other people. They engage in strategies to elicit responses from other people. They can express more emotions, easily cry (because remember, they aren't able to calm themselves; they learn when they cry the other people show up).

These people are afraid of abandonment. They'll engage in strategies to keep people nearby.

They'll rely on others to manage their emotions. They can be clingy and dependent on others for emotional regulation. Now, if I'm dependent on you to make me feel safe, paradoxically, I will tolerate a lot of bad behavior. Even though you're toxic to me, the thought of you leaving me is so terrifying that I'll tolerate bad behavior from you.

Since anxiously attached people believe the world is fundamentally unsafe, it has implications on [[Motivation]]. They aren't intrinsically motivated. The ability to do stuff requires that when I put forth effort, the world gives me some kind of reward. But, if the world is dangerous and scary, it disincentives me from exploring.

Anxiously attached people can sometimes be very bubbly or positive. They have learned that as a hyper attachment strategy to bring people in, if I'm a crying mess, people won't like me and they will leave. So instead, I will be bubbly, positive, and likeable. Because if I'm not likeable then people will leave.

This can be confusing for people in relationships with anxiously attached individuals. On one day, they're upbeat. On another day, they're being passive aggressive; they're clearly upset but not telling me why they're upset. I'm confused to what's going on. This is because, if the world is an inconsistent place, sometimes I need to rapidly change my strategy to get what I want from the world.

Anxiously attached people are capable of intimacy and sometimes even dependent on intimacy. Intimacy means we're close and you're not leaving. They struggle with issues of separation and abandonment and will work hard to avoid those things. Everything from engaging in manipulative behavior to tolerating abuse.

Anxious attachment can be common in divorced / separated couples because the emotional presence of each of the parents can be quite different. Weekends with one parent then the other can be confusing for a three year old.

The key hallmark of anxious attachment: Inconsistency. The rules of the game keep changing. It's confusing. So, you can never relax, because you don't understand how to play the game.

3) **Avoidant Attachment**
When the kid enters the room with the parent, the kid is okay going to play. When stranger enters the room, they don't really care. When the parent leaves, they also don't really care. Parent comes back, the kid ignores the parent. But, some of these kids will express some kind of negative thing toward the parent.
*Example: if the parent says "let's go", the kid is defiant. Or if the parent wants to hold the child's hand, the child will jerk their hand away.*

Something about these kids implies that they're okay without the parent. They're very independent; very comfortable on their own. But, these kids are faking it. Further studies on avoidant attachment show that, even though they appears to be calm, measuring their arousal response (heart rate, blood pressure), the child is panicking. There's a [[Sympathetic Nervous System|SNS]] response which they're masking by remaining cold and numb on the outside. They pretend like everything is okay and that they don't need anyone in this world. But on the inside, their physiology is hyperactivated, just like the anxious child.

In adulthood, people with avoidant attachment are fiercely independent. They've learned that they can't rely on other people. Sometimes, people are nice, loving, and available. Other times, I'm left on my own and I can't predict which one is happening. These people consistently don't get some level of emotional support. They may not have been neglected - maybe one parent was a big proponent of tough love, or had to work a lot and couldn't be around to provide emotional affection. There isn't a lot of emotional support for them. They have to learn to calm down their own emotions. But, they don't really calm them down. What they really do is suppress them.

In their upbringing, they've made signals to others that they need help, but those signals get consistently rebuffed in some way. Eventually they learn that they can't count on others and they have to manage everything themselves.

In adulthood, these people can be like robots. Like machines. They can be high functioning and high performance. It's all about what I do. I have to accomplish everything. I have to build my life into what it is. It is very sort of like an approach of fierce independence and self-reliance, which while it can be laudable in some ways because they're very, very hardworking, it's also kind of sad because these people live in a world where they can't rely on anyone else, where everything falls on their shoulders.

We see robotness or emotional numbness or coldness. They tend to have difficulty with intimacy. Some of them avoid intimacy, because it feels scary to them.

Avoidant attachment is very common. It's extremely common in men due to the way we raise boys and the ideals of masculinity - fierce independence.

4) **Disorganized Attachment**
We see this in the case of severe abuse, neglect, or [[Trauma]].

Their internal environment is extremely chaotic. It can alternate between periods of numbness or intense periods of emotional experience. These people can be very dysregulated.

In the Strange Situation experiment, I'm not even sure that they had a single case of a truly disorganized child. If you want to find cases of disorganized attachment, we have to look at situations like the Romanian orphans, where these were children that were severely neglected & didn't have enough adult supervision. They feel numb. Can become catatonic. They struggle to manage their emotional state.

Disorganized attachment really requires clinical treatment. The things that cause it are severe. This doesn't mean disorganized attachment can't get better, but it requires a lot of hand holding during the painstaking process of really understanding what you're feeling on the inside. They need a lot of support where people can hold your emotions for you. A parent holds the emotional energy for the child so that when they have an explosion, it doesn't actually hurt them, because the parent absorbs it for the child. In the case of disorganized attachment, we need a strong clinical team. Whether it be from loving friends, partners, or clinicians to hold the emotional energy for the person, teach them about what they're feeling, and teach them emotional regulation techniques. Then, there's a slow process of allowing intimacy, vulnerability, and trust. These children learned growing up as a survival mechanism that I cannot trust or be intimate with other people.


When we're struggling with [[Trauma]], the way that we form connections with others can be difficult. We find ourselves repeating these same cycles of either being too clingy or not being able to emotionally connect with someone, not being able to cry with our partner, not being able to be vulnerable, or being a doormat. The cycles we end up in have to do with the way we view relationships. When our brain was wiring what a relationship looks like, there were certain truths that got implanted into your circuitry.

Even though things got wired that way, the brain is plastic, which means we can rewire. Understanding attachment theory and the way we were raised give us insight into why we feel the things that we feel, why we engage in the behaviors that we engage in. Not understanding this can be confusing and disempowering.
