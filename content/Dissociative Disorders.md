Alias: Depersonalization, Dissociative, Disassociation

Dissociative Disorders are a set of disorders characterized by the disruption or discontinuity of the integration of [[Consciousness]], [[Memory]], [[Thought]], [[Emotion]], sensory input, [[Ego|identity]], and physical sensation.

As a human being, you are a constellation of different things. You have thoughts, emotions, memories, a sense of [[Ego|identity]], an experience of [[Consciousness]]; so, you can feel different sensory inputs. You also have internal visceral inputs - physical sensations like heart rate. All of this stuff gets integrated into a human experience. When the continuity of all of these different pieces gets messed up or disrupted in some way, this is when we get a dissociative disorder.

Sensory input - photons hitting the retina, vibrations conducted through the inner ear - that input enters the [[Thalamus]] where they get modulated. Information in your ears goes to the [[Auditory Cortex]]. The auditory association cortex attaches meaning to the sounds. This can trigger thoughts. Then. our [[Limbic system]] injects emotions into the experience. This cascades along - we add our sense of [[Ego|identity]] to the mix. We can even add behavior to the mix. Maybe you'll laugh, text someone, etc.

This is what it means to be human - our experience of life is this constellation of things we put together. When there's a disruption of the **putting together** of these things, that's a dissociative disorder. A lot of illnesses have to do with a single part that is busted on some way. A heart attack is a discrete part of the body that isn't functioning properly (blood flow to the heart). A migraine is a problem with blood flow to particular part of the brain. These are problems with a single instrument. Dissociative disorders are a problem with the **orchestra** - the integration of the individual pieces.

When we use the term dissociation, we're referring to really a harsh unplugging from the world around you. Almost everyone experiences a mild form of dissociation at some point - things like thought suppression, or numbness - are on the pathway to dissociation. Chances are all of these kind of experiences of suppressing, feeling numb, and eventually dissociating are all on a continuum that probably involve different levels of activation of the same parts of the brain. If we activate it mildly, we are suppressing something. If we activate it moderately, we're numbing something. If we activate it on an extreme level, then we're dissociating fully.

**Symptoms of Dissociative Disorders:**
- **Dissociation** - Feeling disconnected to the world.
	- You have a sense of [[Nature of Self|self]], and then there's the world out there. Generally speaking, we are connected to the world. In dissociation, we unplug from **the world**; you feel disconnected.
- **Depersonalization** - Disconnection from your [[Nature of Self|self]]. We feel severed from our selves. People will describe it as feeling like a robot (emotionless). Like, I can observe my actions, but don't feel "connected" to myself.
- Dissociative **Amnesia** - we feel connected to our life through our [[Memory|Memories]]. We forget or become disconnected from our past.

When these experiences become so common that they impair your ability to live life, it qualifies as a dissociative disorder.

A few of the diagnoses:
- Dissociative identity disorders - characterized by dissociation from the outside world.
- Multiple personality disorder - Debated within the psychiatric community. People can have experiences of different fluctuations of the self. But whether we have concrete multiple consciousnesses within a single person - we're not really sure.
- Dissociative Amnesia - you continue to operate, but you have no memory of what you did during that fugue state.

If you think that this is relevant to you or you experience a lot of dissociation or depersonalization, those can all be parts of [[Post Traumatic Stress Disorder|PTSD]] or [[Post Traumatic Stress Disorder#Complex PTSD]].

Over the last 20 years, neuroscience research has discovered a lot about the nature of [[Consciousness]] and experiences that are not associated with mental illness, like out-of-body experiences or transcendental states in [[Meditation]].

There are some **parts of the brain** which give us our sense of [[Nature of Self|self]] which are implicated in these disorders.
- Activating the [[Temporoparietal Junction]] (such as electrically), can trigger some of these dissociative experiences, depersonalization, as well as out-of-body experiences.
- When you think about yourself, the [[Default Mode Network]] is active. Something around the [[Default Mode Network|DMN]] is associated with depersonalization, dissociation, and especially [[Trauma]].
	- Hyperactivity of the [[Default Mode Network|DMN]] can create [[Depression]], where we're constantly thinking about ourself. One of the treatments for [[Depression]] is [[Ketamine]], which induces dissociation. When we separate ourselves from our [[Default Mode Network|DMN]], we can stop (temporarily) stop [[Depression]] in its tracks.

Dissociative disorders seem to map on [[Spirituality|Spiritual]] experiences. Practices from spiritual traditions can induce dissociation-like states. In these spiritual traditions, we will trigger something called a [[Transcendental|Transcendental State of Consciousness]]. We also see this to a certain degree in [[Psychedelics]].

Substances that work in our body, activate existing structures. For example, opiate medication works because we already have mu receptors, which are the receptors that trigger pain relief. Opiate medication bind to those receptors and turn on this circuit in our brain.

For thousands of years, human beings have been triggering some kind of dissociative or depersonalized state through practices like [[Meditation]]. There are studies that show that when you meditate regularly, you're more happy. And the reason is because we're able to let things go. When you [[Vairagya|detach]] from something in life, it allows you to experience life in the moment without [[Attachment]]s. [[Attachment]] to [[Desire]] causes [[Dukkha|suffering]].

*Example: I eat a cookie. I get some pleasure, but now I have a desire for a second cookie. I eat a second cookie. Now I feel guilty because of my attachment to my physical form - 'I'll get fat'.* 
Even engaging in pleasurable things can be uncomfortable. The [[Attachment]] to the [[Desire]] to look sexy creates the unhappiness of eating the second cookie.

Children have fewer attachments than adults. If we free ourselves of attachments, we can experience the joys of life very simply without the consequences that we attach to them.

Detachment is a separating ourselves from our attachments - the importance we attach to various things around us. Attachment to [[Ego]]. In dissociation, we unplug from the **experience itself**. It's the opposite of detachment, even though it uses a similar mechanism. In datachment. we can throw ourselves into an experience without worrying about the consequences. We can be completeley present. This is the goal of [[Meditation]] - to be one with the object of the meditation or the universe or whatever.

Dissociation is probably a survival mechanism to help organisms do things like play dead. When we feel an overwhelming amount of physical or emotional pain, we can't deal with it, so our brain just shuts things off completely. Animals who play dead's reflexes are suppressed. It isn't something that's consciously controlled.

But, if I am in a relatively healthy space and I somehow activate this machinery, some people will describe these transcendental experiences as transformative, as healing, as blissful. We're not quite sure exactly what's going on, but chances are we're sort of triggering something in the [[Temporoparietal Junction]] and disabling the [[Default Mode Network|DMN]]. There's a history in the [[Spirituality|Spiritual]] traditions of cultivating these kinds of states for the purpose of [[Spirituality|Spiritual]] growth.
